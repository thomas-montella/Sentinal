🧲 MAGNET & MOTOR SUBSTITUTES
Replaces:
NdFeB Rare Earth Magnets
Neodymium-Iron-Boron (China ~94% processing)
Substitute:
Iron Nitride (Fe16N2)
TRL 5–6 · Niron Magnetics (Minneapolis, MN)
IN PROGRESS
2027–2029 automotive grade
2030–2032 defense qualification
🌟 MOST STRATEGICALLY IMPORTANT SUBSTITUTE IN THIS DATABASE. Iron Nitride magnets are made from iron + atmospheric nitrogen — two of the most abundant materials on earth. Zero rare earth content = zero China dependency for magnets. Theoretical performance: 18% higher magnetization than best NdFeB. Stable to 200°C. Niron Magnetics broke ground on its Sartell, MN manufacturing facility in September 2025. Stellantis partnership + DoE $2.7M AMMTO grant. If successful, this eliminates China's permanent magnet chokehold over F-35 actuators, submarine motors, missile guidance, and every other REE-magnet-dependent system.
Performance: ~85–95% vs NdFeB (comparable for most applications)
Cost: ~30–50% lower (abundant inputs)
Funding: DoE ARPA-E REACT + Stellantis + Anzu Partners VC
DoD Endorsed: DoE funded; DoD monitoring closely
Replaces:
Samarium-Cobalt Magnets
High-temp magnets for missiles/aerospace
Substitute:
Advanced Ferrite + FeN Hybrids
TRL 3–4 · Univ. of Delaware / DoE CMI
LONG TIMELINE
2032–2035 defense-grade
SmCo magnets are harder to replace than NdFeB because extreme-temperature performance is a genuine technical constraint. Terbium-doped NdFeB is the current high-temp solution — itself China-dependent. Iron Nitride + specialty coating solutions are the long-term path. Near-term strategy: stockpile SmCo. Medium-term: fund high-temp magnet alternatives aggressively via DoD/DoE partnership.
Performance: ~70–80% at high temperature (gap vs SmCo in extreme environments)
Cost: ~20–40% lower long-term
Funding: DoE CMM Institute grant; limited DoD
📡 SEMICONDUCTOR & ELECTRONICS SUBSTITUTES
Replaces:
Gallium (GaN) in Radar
GaN T/R modules — F-35 AESA, AN/TPY-2, SPY-6 (BAN ACTIVE)
Partial substitute only:
Silicon Carbide (SiC) — power apps only
SiC TRL 7–8 · Wolfspeed (US, post-bankruptcy)
CRITICAL GAP
SiC: available now (power only)
GaN T/R radar: NO substitute <5yrs
⚠️ CRITICAL GAP: There is NO near-term substitute for GaN in high-frequency radar T/R modules. SiC covers power electronics but cannot replace GaN for RF/microwave radar applications. Gallium Oxide (Ga2O3) is promising but is a decade away from defense qualification. The only viable strategies are: (1) ally-source gallium from Germany/India, (2) stockpile pre-ban gallium inventory, (3) develop low-gallium or gallium-recycling radar architectures as long-term R&D. Every US AESA radar is vulnerable until this gap is closed.
SiC for power: ~80% equivalent (viable for EV/energy not radar RF)
Ga2O3 (future): TRL 3–4, potentially cost-competitive, but 2030+ timeline
Wolfspeed CHIPS Act: $750M (post-bankruptcy) for US SiC capacity
Replaces:
Germanium in IR Optics
IR focal plane arrays — Javelin seeker, thermal sights, satellite sensors (BAN ACTIVE)
Substitute:
Zinc Selenide (ZnSe) / Chalcogenide Glass
TRL 6–7 · II-VI (Coherent), Umicore
VIABLE PATH
Available now for new designs
3–5 yrs to requalify existing systems
ZnSe and chalcogenide glass are viable alternatives for many IR optic applications — ~85–90% performance for the 8–12 micron band used by thermal sights and missile seekers. The key challenge is system requalification. Existing Javelin, FLIR systems, and satellite sensors are qualified around germanium optics. Redesigning and requalifying to new materials takes 3–5 years and significant cost. Near-term strategy: New designs (future Javelin successor, next-gen FLIR) should be designed around Ge-alternatives from the start.
Performance: ZnSe ~85–90% for 8–12μm band; silicon ~70% for near-IR only
Cost: ZnSe 2–3x more expensive than Ge currently
Funding: Commercial R&D; some DARPA IR optics programs
Replaces:
Advanced Logic Chips ≤5nm
TSMC Taiwan produces 90%+ — $2.7T global GDP loss if disrupted
Strategy:
CHIPS Act Domestic Fab Buildout
TSMC AZ Fab 1 online · Intel OH (delayed) · $30.9B invested
IN PROGRESS
AZ Fab 1: NOW (limited)
US target: 20% share by 2030
The CHIPS Act is the right policy — but the gap is 8–10 years. TSMC Arizona Fab 1 is operational but tiny vs. Taiwan capacity. Fabs 2–6 run to 2030. US will reach ~20% of leading-edge chip capacity by 2030 — still 80% Taiwan/ally dependent. The H-1B visa fee ($100K, Sep 2025) is slowing TSMC Arizona engineering hires — a self-inflicted wound. This is a managed-decline-of-dependency problem, not a solved problem. Intelligence conclusion: CHIPS Act progress is real but insufficient for a 2025-2030 Taiwan conflict scenario.
US share 2022: 0%
US target 2030: ~20% leading-edge
CHIPS Act total: $30.9B direct funding (GAO-26-107882, Dec 2025)
19 companies, 40 projects: as of Jul 2025
Replaces:
ASML EUV Equipment
100% global monopoly on EUV — required for ALL ≤7nm chips
No substitute exists:
Alliance Management Only
No US/allied alternative planned · TRL: N/A
NO SUBSTITUTE
No substitute path identified
within 10 years
THE MOST CONCENTRATED SINGLE POINT OF FAILURE IN ALL OF TECHNOLOGY. ASML in Eindhoven, Netherlands produces 100% of all EUV lithography machines globally. Zero alternative exists or is being developed by any nation. Deep UV (DUV) lithography limits chips to ≥7nm — insufficient for advanced defense AI and guidance systems. The only risk management strategies are: (1) maintain strong US-Netherlands NATO alliance, (2) enforce China EUV export controls (US-NL cooperation ongoing), (3) diversify fab geography so ASML tools are distributed across trusted nations. There is no substitute — not in 5 years, not in 10 years. This is permanent geopolitical leverage.
DUV alternative: Limits production to ≥7nm — inadequate for advanced defense computing
Alliance status: US-NL semiconductor partnership active; China EUV export controls enforced
Strategic conclusion: Protect ASML access; no technology solution exists
🚀 PROPELLANT & ENERGETIC SUBSTITUTES
Replaces:
Hydrazine / MMH
Satellite/missile RCS thrusters — ALL US satellites and ICBMs
Substitute:
AF-M315E "ASCENT" Green Propellant
TRL 8–9 · Aerojet Rocketdyne · Flown in space 2019
MATURE SUBSTITUTE
Available NOW
DoD qualifying for defense sats
✅ BEST NEWS IN THIS TABLE: A viable, flight-proven hydrazine alternative already exists. Aerojet's AF-M315E "ASCENT" propellant flew successfully on NASA's GPIM mission and multiple commercial satellites. Higher specific impulse (+12% vs hydrazine), lower toxicity, lower handling/storage costs, less infrastructure needed. DoD (AFRL/SMC) actively qualifying for defense satellite programs. This should be a priority transition — eliminates hydrazine storage risk and regulatory exposure. ETA for broad DoD adoption: 2026–2028.
Performance: +12% specific impulse vs hydrazine — better, not just equivalent
Cost: ~25–30% higher propellant cost; lower handling/infrastructure cost
Flight heritage: NASA GPIM mission (2019) + 12+ commercial satellites
DoD status: AFRL actively qualifying for small sats + larger platforms
Replaces:
Cotton-Linter Nitrocellulose
Gun propellant base — 70% dependent on Chinese cotton linters
Substitute:
Wood-Pulp Based Nitrocellulose
TRL 6–7 · Rheinmetall (EU lead) · GD OTS (US)
NEAR-TERM VIABLE
2–3 years to scale at
defense-grade quality
Wood-pulp NC is technically viable and near-equivalent in performance (~90–95%). The challenge is production scale and quality consistency for defense-grade propellant. Rheinmetall acquired German NC producer Hagedorn-NC in April 2025 specifically to convert to military-grade production — Europe is leading the US on this. The US is behind. DoD should fund US wood-pulp NC capacity immediately — this is a 2–3 year fix if funded, vs. a 10-year problem if ignored. Eliminating Chinese cotton linter dependency for NC is achievable and affordable.
Performance: ~90–95% equivalent (near-parity)
Cost: Roughly equivalent or slightly higher
Rheinmetall action: Acquired Hagedorn-NC Apr 2025 for military-grade conversion
US status: Behind Europe; needs immediate DoD funding
Replaces:
Ammonium Perchlorate (AP)
Sole-source solid rocket oxidizer — ALL US solid-fuel missiles
Future substitute:
Ammonium Dinitramide (ADN)
TRL 4–5 · FOI/Eurenco (Sweden lead) · AFRL research
LONG TIMELINE
2030–2035 defense qualification
Near-term: BUILD 2ND FACILITY
AP substitute is a 10+ year problem. ADN (Ammonium Dinitramide) is less toxic and more energetically efficient than AP but costs more and is unproven at scale in defense systems. AFRL has research programs. Swedish FOI leads ADN development. The near-term answer is not a substitute — it is: BUILD A SECOND AP PRODUCTION FACILITY. The medium-term answer is fund ADN qualification aggressively. Until either happens, AMPAC Cedar City is the single most dangerous chokepoint in US defense manufacturing — one fire stops all missile production.
ADN performance: ~90% of AP specific impulse; lower toxicity; more expensive
HAN-based propellants: ~85% performance; even less mature
Near-term priority: AMPAC $100M expansion (Apr 2025) — necessary but insufficient
Strategic conclusion: Second AP facility is the only real near-term solution
🔋 BATTERY & MATERIALS SUBSTITUTES
Replaces:
Chinese Spherical Graphite
Li-ion battery anodes — China 100% of global processing
Substitute:
Synthetic Graphite / Silicon Anode Composites
TRL 6–8 · NanoGraf (DoD-funded, Chicago) · Group14 (Woodinville WA)
IN PROGRESS
Synthetic: 2–3 years to scale
Silicon anode: limited use 2025–2027
Synthetic graphite and silicon-composite anodes are viable paths to eliminating China's 100% spherical graphite monopoly. NanoGraf (Chicago) is DoD-funded specifically for silicon anode materials for defense batteries. Group14 (Woodinville, WA) produces silicon anode materials with Boeing and DoD support. The challenge: cost (3–5x) and scaling. The CHIPS Act model — government offtake + price floor + equity — should be replicated immediately for battery anodes. Silicon anodes also offer a performance advantage: +20–40% energy density vs. graphite, which matters for UAV endurance.
Synthetic graphite: ~95% equivalent energy density; 3–5x more expensive
Silicon anode advantage: +20–40% energy density vs graphite — better performance, not just equivalent
DoD funding: NanoGraf direct DoD investment for defense battery applications
Group14: Boeing + DoD support; Woodinville WA facility scaling
Replaces:
Cobalt in Jet Engine Superalloys
Turbine blades — F-135 (F-35), F-119 (F-22), all military jet engines
Substitute:
Ceramic Matrix Composites (CMC)
TRL 7–8 · GE Aviation (deployed in F414 / LEAP) · Already flying
PROVEN TECHNOLOGY
CMC: available NOW
Broader military use: 3–5 yrs requalify
✅ GOOD NEWS: GE has already deployed CMC turbine blades in commercial engines (LEAP, GE9X) and military (F414). CMC operates at higher temperatures than cobalt superalloys with lower weight — technically superior, not just equivalent. The F-35's F-135 engine (Pratt & Whitney) does not yet use CMC in the hot section — transition requires 3–5 year requalification but is technically proven. CMC eliminates cobalt dependency in engine hot sections, removing the DRC/China supply chain risk for the most critical structural engine components.
Performance: CMC comparable or better at max temperature — lighter weight too
GE deployment: LEAP (commercial), GE9X, F414 (military) — flight proven
F-35 transition: Requires 3–5 year requalification of F-135 hot section
DoD status: YES — GE already deploying in military engines
